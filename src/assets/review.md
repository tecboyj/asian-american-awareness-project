In Jade City, Fonda Lee weaves an elaborate fantasy about power, family, and loyalty, while she resists the narratives historically imposed on Asian and Asian American writers. She challenges the perpetual foreigner and model minority stereotypes by creating a society influenced by Chinese culture without explaining it through a western perspective, exemplifying Cathy Park Hong’s call to “overhaul the tired ethnic narratives that have automated our identities” (Park 47). This, in turn, rejects expectations of the American publishing industry and demonstrates that stories rooted in Asian culture can appeal to a broad audience without sacrificing authenticity.

From the onset of Jade City, readers are immersed in the fictional island nation of Kekon through the experiences of the No Peak clan, a powerful family whose influence depends on jade, a substance that grants enhanced magical abilities. Lee’s inspiration from East Asian cultures is felt by the readers not by explanation or justification of these cultural elements but through the development of the characters. Instead, she presents a society with its own history, politics, and moral codes trusting the readers to engage with an unfamiliar cultural framework. Thus, the readers are invited into a world that is unique, unfamiliar, and authentic.

The reader is the foreigner immersed in a different culture, the outsider, the immigrant in the Kekon society. The perpetual foreigner stereotype assumes that Asian people are never fully accepted as belonging, even when they are citizens or have lived in a country for generations. Lee does not allow this to exist in her novel. Lee places the reader in the role of the cultural newcomer, refusing to translate every cultural reference. This reversal challenges the expectation that Asian stories must be framed through translation or cultural explanation to be accessible. This is a refreshing experience for the reader.

The novel’s characters further resist limiting stereotypes. The Kaul family, leaders of the No Peak clan, are ambitious, violent, flawed, and deeply human. Characters like Kaul Hilo and Kaul Shae make morally conflicted decisions, engage in brutal conflicts, and struggle with questions of power and identity. Their complexity directly counters the model minority stereotype, which often portrays Asians to be compliant and quiet. The Kaul family are dynamic protagonists that drive the narrative.

This resistance aligns closely with Cathy Park Hong’s assertion in Minor Feelings: “I also hope that we can seize the opportunity and change American literature completely. Overhaul the tired ethnic narratives that have automated our identities; that have made our lives palatable to a white audience but removed them from our own lived realities—and stop spelling ourselves out in the alphabet given to us” (Park 49). Lee’s novel embodies this vision. She does not “spell out” her world according to conventional expectations, nor does she make her characters palatable to a mainstream audience She writes through her experiences and takes the reader along for the ride exploring and seeing Asian culture through a new fresh lens.

Lee breaks free from the publishing industry’s tendency to confine Asian authors to a narrow set of themes. Jade City is not an immigrant story, nor is it a tale of cultural assimilation. It is an epic fantasy rooted in Asian influences, demonstrating that Asian writers can tell any kind of story. Through its intricate setting, complex characters and the continuous plot twists, Lee not only entertains but also expands the possibilities of representation in contemporary literature, answering Hong’s call to transform American literature from within.
